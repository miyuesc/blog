# 什么可以帮助你在前端面试中取得成功（以及什么可能会阻碍你）

>
> 作者：[Dzmitry Ihnatovich](https://medium.com/@ignatovich.dm)



> The frontend market in 2025 is tough — but not impossible. You just need to know how to play it smart.



I've been navigating the job market recently. Some interviews went really well. Others… not so much.

After enough of these experiences (plus asking for feedback whenever I could), I started noticing some patterns.

So I'm writing this post for anyone else out there who's job-hunting in frontend right now. Hopefully, some of these lessons can save you time, help you prepare smarter, or just remind you that you're not alone in this process.

Let's get into it 👇

### ✅ What Could Help You Stand Out

#### 1. Strong Fundamentals > Fancy Libraries

Look, I love a good React project as much as the next person — but you know what really catches my eye?

Someone who understands:

- JavaScript (especially ES6+, async/await, closures, types)
- How the **event loop** works in JavaScript
- Why **CSS specificity** matters
- The difference between `==` and `===`
- What **semantic HTML** actually means

If you can walk person through those clearly, you're already got a lot of bonus points.

> *💡* Interviewers still ask vanilla JS and CSS questions. Don't rely on frameworks to carry you.

#### 2. Building Projects That I Could Talk About

At first, my portfolio was full of "typical" tutorials — todo lists, calculators, the usual suspects. But the moment I added a small personal project, everything changed.

Interviewers started asking about:

- my design decisions
- my folder structure
- how I handled edge cases
- what I'd improve next

And I had real answers, because I actually cared about the thing I built.

> *💡 Tip: It doesn't need to be huge or perfect — just real. Something you built from scratch that you can speak passionately about.*

#### 3. Learning to Think Out Loud

One thing I totally underestimated: how much they care about **your thought process**.

Even when I didn't get the perfect solution, just explaining how I was approaching the problem helped me move forward in interviews. I'd say things like:

> *"I'm not 100% sure, but I think I'd try X first and validate with a console log."*

Or:

> *"This might not scale well, so maybe we could refactor it later using a reducer."*

And that kind of honest, transparent thinking helped build trust.

> *💡 Tip: Even if you're unsure, walk them through your thinking. Silence is worse than imperfection.*

#### 4. Being Curious About the Ecosystem

You don't need to know every new tool, but when I started showing curiosity about:

- React Server Components
- performance optimization
- testing
- deployment (just basic Vercel/Netlify stuff)

…it made me stand out. Even if I wasn't an expert, interviewers appreciated that I was **interested** and **growing**.

> *💡 Tip: Stay curious. Even knowing how to talk about a concept (like hydration or lazy loading) can make you look more senior.*

### 🚩 What Can Held You Back

Alright, let's talk about what didn't go so well — so you can avoid these if you can.

#### ❌ 1. No Public Work to Show (already mentioned)

#### ❌ 2. Overloading My Resume With Buzzwords

At one point, my resume said something like:

> *"Skilled in React, Vue, Angular, Svelte, Node, GraphQL, Tailwind, Webpack…"*

Guess how many of those I'd actually used in production? Maybe three. And it backfired.

In one interview, I was asked a detailed Vue question. I froze. It wasn't malicious, but I'd misled them.

> *💡 Tip: List what you've used meaningfully. It's better to go deep on a few tools than pretend you've used everything.*

#### ❌ 3. Messy Code Submissions

When I rushed take-home tasks, I'd end up submitting code that "worked" but wasn't clean — bad variable names, no comments, unclear structure.

I now realize that most teams care more about **how** you write code than whether it's perfect. They're thinking: *Would I want to review or maintain this code?*

> *💡 Tip: Always leave time to clean up your code. Read it like someone else has to maintain it.*

#### ❌ 4. Not Preparing Questions for Them

I used to think the "Do you have any questions for us?" part was just a formality. Big mistake.

Now I always ask things like:

- "What does your code review process look like?"
- "What tech debt are you currently dealing with?"
- "How do designers and devs collaborate on your team?"

That's when the interview turns into a real conversation. And more than once, interviewers have told me:

> *"That's a great question — no one ever asks that."*

#### ❌ 5. Being Too Hard on Myself

I've had interviews where I blanked on something basic. I've had days where I was sure I blew it.

But beating myself up never helped. What *did* help was taking notes, improving one small thing for next time, and moving on.

Sometimes it's not even about you — team fit, budget shifts, timing… it's not always in your control.

> *💡 Reminder: Rejections suck, but they're also normal. Use them as feedback, not as judgment.*

#### ❌ 6. Negative Energy About Past Teams

One of the quickest ways to lose an interviewer is to start trashing your former coworkers or managers.

Saying things like:

> *"My last team didn't know what they were doing" "The company was a total mess"*

Some time ago I failed a cultural fit interview because I said that my former manager asked me to use AI in my work as much as possible, and I was not happy. I didn't provide any constructive reason, so…

…makes us wonder: *Are you going to be a problem here too?* Even if you had a rough experience, **keep it constructive**.

### 🧠 A Few Final Tips That Could Really Help

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*A-cTCyIfRqT__RT-.gif)

- **Practice mock interviews**, especially live coding and "think aloud" exercises.
- **Prepare a few smart questions** for your interviewer (ask about the team, code review process, how they ship code).
- **Update your LinkedIn and GitHub**!
- **Talk about your learning**: bootcamps, self-study, books, YouTube, blog posts… we love seeing initiative.
- **Use AI wisely**. It's a great tool for learning and debugging — but if your answers sound AI-generated and you can't explain them? That's a red flag.

### 🚀 Final Thoughts

The frontend world is evolving fast. It's not just about building UIs anymore — it's about how you think, how you grow, and how you **work with others**.

If you focus on:

- Strong fundamentals
- Real projects
- Honest communication
- A learning mindset

…you're already on a great path.

You don't have to be perfect. Just be **prepared**, **curious**, and **real**.
